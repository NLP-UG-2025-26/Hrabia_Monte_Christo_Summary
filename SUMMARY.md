Introduction
Text can be approached from several different angles. Some treat it purely as a linguistic structure shaped by context, while discourse analysts see it as both linguistic and social. It means that a newspaper article, for instance, is shaped by broader media conventions rather than just the sentences it contains. Van Dijk and Kintsch take a different approach, focusing on the cognitive architecture of text, distinguishing between local sentence-level connections and the broader organisational layout. Halliday and Hasan offer a third view, treating text as language fulfilling a purpose in context, held together by both structural unity and cohesive linguistic ties, though they note that cohesion alone does not guarantee coherence.
Register or Genre?
The terms register, genre, and text type are frequently mixed up. A register refers to recurring language patterns tied to a specific situation, while a genre is a culturally recognised category of text. Registers range from broad to highly specific sub-registers, and even sub-registers within the same area can differ significantly in their language use. Different corpora classify these sub-registers in different ways. The ICE uses more explicit layers, while the BNC groups more sub-registers under wider categories. Both systems have limitations, but remain useful frameworks.
Speech and Writing
Speech and writing are often treated as opposites, but Biber (1988) argues they sit on a continuum. Using factor analysis, he identified six dimensions of variation across texts, showing that language features cluster together according to communicative function. Formal registers like academic writing and official documents tend toward informational density, using nominalisations and agentless passives, while casual conversation relies more on interactive features like personal pronouns.
Unity of Structure
Halliday and Hasan distinguish between closed registers, which follow a fixed predictable structure, and open registers, which are more loosely organised. A service encounter is a clear example of a closed register, moving through set stages regardless of exact wording. Spontaneous conversation, by contrast, is far more flexible, though still governed by loose turn-taking conventions. The following section examines three text types: spontaneous dialogues, telephone conversations, and press reportage, each sitting at a different point on this structural spectrum.
Spoken Registers
Since speech is our main form of communication, it is worth examining its main registers in English. The ICE classifies spoken language by whether it is public or private, and monologic or dialogic. These categories are not perfect: mobile phones have blurred the line between private and public calls, and some registers like broadcast news mix scripted and spontaneous speech, but they remain useful for understanding how spoken texts are structured. One of the most studied spoken registers is spontaneous dialogue. The foundational work of Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson (1974) was among the first to systematically describe how speakers manage turn-taking, relying on transcriptions of recorded speech to capture features like speaker turns, utterances, and pauses.

Structure of Spoken Texts
Spontaneous conversation is one of the most common spoken registers. According to Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson (1974), conversations are organised through turn-taking. Usually only one person speaks at a time, overlaps are brief, and speakers have considerable freedom in choosing topics, turn length and speaking order. Although conversations are generally flexible, their openings and closings tend to follow recognisable patterns.
Many conversations begin with greetings such as Hi or Hey, often followed by questions about a person's wellbeing or recent activities. These exchanges are examples of adjacency pairs, where one utterance naturally prompts a specific response. Such openings are often forms of phatic communication, serving a social rather than informational purpose by maintaining relationships between speakers.
Telephone conversations have a more structured opening because participants must first establish contact and identify one another. Typically, a phone call begins with a summons-answer sequence, where the ringing phone prompts a response such as Hello. Depending on familiarity and context, speakers may identify themselves or immediately continue the conversation. Modern technologies such as caller ID and mobile phones have reduced the need for formal identification during calls.
Conversation closings are also organised. Speakers often use pre-closing sequences, such as expressing pleasure at the interaction or making future plans, before exchanging farewells. These stages help participants end conversations smoothly and avoid abrupt or awkward endings.
Turn-Taking in Conversation
The middle section of a conversation is generally less structured and is governed primarily by turn-taking. Speaker turns can vary greatly in length, ranging from single words to complete sentences. At the end of each unit of speech there is a transition relevance place (TRP), a point where another speaker may take a turn.
Speakers can select the next speaker directly, often through questions or other adjacency pairs that require a response. If no speaker is selected, another participant may self-select and begin speaking. This sometimes results in overlapping speech, though overlaps are usually resolved quickly.
Conversations also contain repair mechanisms. In a self-initiated repair, speakers correct or reformulate their own speech. In an other-initiated repair, another speaker helps clarify or complete what someone is trying to say. Another common feature is the use of back channels such as mhm or yeah. These signals show attentiveness and support without interrupting the main speaker or attempting to take over the conversation.
Written Registers
Written registers can be classified into a variety of categories depending on purpose, audience and subject matter. Systems such as the British National Corpus distinguish between academic and non-academic prose, fiction, essays, letters and different types of newspapers. Each register contains further sub-registers with their own linguistic characteristics.
The boundaries between registers are not always clear. Academic disciplines often overlap, and many texts display features of multiple registers at once. In addition, developments in technology have introduced new written forms such as blogs and expanded the role of email, while traditional handwritten letters have become less common. These changes demonstrate that written registers continue to evolve alongside changing communication practices.
